RoboMop New Era:
an autonomous mop robot
and the plan to deliver it

An autonomous floor-cleaning robot engineered around everything its predecessor taught us — and the 16-week plan to deliver it

RoboMop New Era maps and cleans large floors on its own for an estimated 4.8 hours at its hardest workload, swaps its battery in under five minutes without rebooting, and protects people and itself with safety that lives in hardware, not software. It irrigates as it cleans, sees with LiDAR and two cameras, and reports to a fleet app. We deliver it in 16 weeks: one fully verified robot, one LFP accumulator, and the documentation to build ten more.

§3 · How it works

How the robot is organized

One brain thinks, one brain drives — and the driving one answers to hardware, not to the other computer's mood.

The thinking brain handles maps, routes, cameras and the app. The driving brain runs the motors a thousand times a second and timestamps every sensor against a single clock, so the map the robot builds never drifts. If the thinking brain ever hangs, the driving brain stops the motors by itself — the stop is wired in silicon, and no software bug can override it. Water delivery, battery swap and the emergency-stop chain all answer to the same supervised controller, and an automotive-style CAN bus is already in place for accessories we add later.

§4 · Battery and runtime

Battery and runtime

The battery is sized by an energy model built from measured draws — not by optimism. The estimate clears the acceptance bar with room to spare.

The robot's 960 Wh LFP pack is sized against everything it actually draws: the computers, the traction and the cleaning head. At a sustained 200-watt average draw — the worst-case workload we test against — the pack delivers an estimated 4.8 hours of cleaning, clearing the ≥4.0-hour acceptance bar with a 20% margin. When the pack does run down, a fresh one slides in in under five minutes while the controls stay awake — and with one charged spare on the shelf the estimate stretches to 9.6 hours, past a full eight-hour shift, without rebooting or re-mapping. The pack chemistry is LFP — the safe, long-cycle-life choice — in a 24 V · 40 Ah assembly with its own battery-management system.

§5 · The 16-week plan

The 16-week schedule

Requirements freeze in Week 1, the design in Week 2, features in Week 12 — and every Friday you see a demo, not a slide.

The plan runs requirements → design → concurrent engineering → subsystem builds → integration → verification → acceptance. Each of the seven gates has a written criterion and your sign-off: nothing proceeds on promises. If anything on the critical path slips more than three working days, you get a recovery plan immediately — and two consecutive missed weeks trigger a scope or resource review with you, not a quiet slip.

§6 · What could go wrong — and how it's covered

Main risks and responses

We'd rather show you the risk register than pretend there isn't one. Every risk has an objective trigger that forces action — reviewed with you weekly.

The two biggest threats are the ones every ambitious hardware project faces: time and team size. They're met with frozen decision points, parallel workstreams and a 20% rework reserve. Below them, every technical risk — late boards, battery data, water near electronics — has a named engineering response. One rule sits above all others: a single uncontrolled-motion event stops all work until it's understood.

§7 · How you'll know it works

Testing and acceptance

The test matrix is frozen with you in Week 1 — before any results exist — so Week 16 is a demonstration, not a negotiation.

Every capability in this proposal maps to a numbered commitment with a written success criterion. Testing climbs five levels — software simulation, bench rigs, individual subsystems, the integrated robot, and finally a witnessed acceptance run of the frozen matrix. Simulation supports development; it never substitutes for physical proof of runtime, temperature, connectors, battery swap, braking, leaks or electrical safety.

What "accepted" means on delivery day

  • At least 4 hours of continuous cleaning from a full charge under the agreed 200-watt workload — no battery trips, no overheating, no reboots.
  • Battery swap in five minutes or less, with the robot's controls awake throughout; connectors cool and solid.
  • Zero open critical defects, and zero open major defects without your written sign-off.
  • The full autonomy, perception, irrigation and connectivity matrix executed and signed — plus the robot, accumulator, charger, software and complete technical data package in your hands.

And because you may want more than one: every critical assembly carries a part number, revision, serial identification, calibration and pass/fail record, so the tests we run on this robot become the production checks for the next ten.
